What it is

A strength block paired with a dedicated power session, every single week

Phase 2 runs four sessions a week — a heavy strength day and a dedicated power session for both press and pull patterns. Each strength session builds toward a new working max; each power session trains the same pattern at maximum speed instead of maximum load. Squat heavy on Monday, jump explosively on Tuesday — same movement, different intent.

Week 7 opens with a ramping test to establish fresh working maxes. From there, box jumps, med ball throws, banded countermovement jumps, and power cleans get layered in alongside the strength work. Week 12 closes the block with a full re-test — squat, bench, trap bar deadlift, and seal row all pushed to genuine new maxes.

Why it's built this way

Strength without speed is wasted. Speed without strength gets you hurt

A strong squat means nothing on the field if you can't express that strength quickly. That's the entire premise of Phase 2 — every heavy lift is paired with an explosive version of the same pattern, so the strength you've built actually shows up as speed, not just a bigger number on a bar.

This is where most programs get it wrong — they either keep grinding heavy strength work indefinitely, or they jump to plyometrics too early and athletes get hurt landing box jumps they weren't ready for. Phase 2 pairs the two deliberately, in the same session, so your nervous system learns to convert load into speed.

Why this matters

The field doesn't reward how much you can lift slowly

Every sprint, jump, tackle, and change of direction happens in under half a second. A strong lift built over three seconds under the bar doesn't transfer to that automatically — the nervous system has to be trained separately to produce force fast, not just produce it eventually.

That's the gap Phase 2 closes. Six weeks of pairing load with speed, so the strength from Phase 1 stops being a gym number and starts being something you can actually use on the field.
